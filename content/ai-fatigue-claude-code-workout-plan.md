We are living through the golden age of software engineering. Or at least that is what the venture capitalists on the All-In podcast keep telling us between bouts of laughing at poor people. If you look at the metrics, they are right. We are shipping more code than ever before. We are closing Jira tickets at a velocity that would make a scrum master weep tears of pure burndown chart joy. Pixels are being pushed so fast they are practically breaking the sound barrier.

So why does everyone look like they haven’t slept since GPT-3 dropped?

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/9766a984-c759-445e-bf34-a22c6cd829dc_3000x1637.png)

Welcome to the paradox of AI fatigue. It is the dirty little secret lurking in the slack channels of high-performing engineering teams. We were promised a future where we would be the architects, conducting a symphony of AI agents doing the grunt work. Instead, we have become exhausted factory foremen running up and down an infinite assembly line, frantically stamping approval on pull requests generated by a machine that never sleeps, never drinks coffee, and never stops to ask if we are okay.

Siddhant Khare, a software engineer who recently went viral for bravely admitting he is tired of winning, nailed this feeling in his recent essay. He noted that he shipped more code last quarter than in any quarter of his career. Yet he also felt more drained than at any point in his career. These two facts are not unrelated. They are the cause and effect of a new kind of burnout that is specific to the era of large language models.

The core of the problem is a fundamental shift in what it means to be a software engineer. *We used to be creators. We stared at a blank editor, built a mental model of the problem, and typed out the solution.* It was hard work, but it was a single stream of thought. You could enter a flow state. You could put on your noise-canceling headphones and live inside the matrix of your own logic for hours.

*Now we are reviewers. We are editors. We are the human-in-the-loop whose primary job is to catch the subtle hallucinations of a stochastic parrot before they crash production.* Khare describes it perfectly when he says it feels like being a judge at an assembly line that never ends. You just keep stamping those PRs. The machine generates the code in seconds, but it takes you ten minutes to verify it isn’t importing a package that doesn’t exist or subtly introducing a race condition that will only manifest on Black Friday.

This brings us to the physical manifestation of this mental toll, something the internet has affectionately dubbed the **Claude Code Workout Plan**.

The workflow of an AI-assisted engineer is a strange mix of hyper-speed and agonizing paralysis. You type a prompt. You hit enter. And then you wait. Sometimes it takes ten seconds. Sometimes, if the model is “thinking” hard or the inference API is getting hammered, it takes forty-five. It is that awkward duration that is too short to go make coffee but too long to just stare at the screen without losing your mind.

So you tab over to X. You check the price of Bitcoin. You doomscroll for thirty seconds. Then you tab back, realize the code is done, paste it in, see an error, and prompt again. Rinse and repeat. This micro-switching fries your dopamine receptors faster than a TikTok binge.

The Claude Code workout plan proposes a solution. Since we are shackled to our desks waiting for the oracle to speak, we might as well get shredded.

*When you ask for a simple refactor, that is a ten-second wall sit.When you ask for a complex architectural explanation, that is a set of twenty pushups.*

When the model starts hallucinating and you have to argue with it about the existence of a Python library that it is absolutely convinced is real, that is high-intensity interval training. You can scream into a pillow for cardio.

It is a funny concept, but it highlights a serious issue. Our work patterns have become jagged. We are no longer driving the car; we are the driving instructor, constantly grabbing the wheel because the student is driving brilliantly one second and trying to merge into oncoming traffic the next. It is terrifying and exhausting.

The industry is starting to wake up to this. We are realizing that productivity is not just lines of code. It is sustainable output. If you burn out your senior engineers by turning them into PR-stamping robots, you haven’t gained efficiency. You have just borrowed technical debt against your team’s mental health.

There is a future where we figure this out. A future where we use AI to handle the drudgery so we can focus on the creative, high-level thinking that actually matters. But we are not there yet. Right now, we are in the messy transition phase. We are the test pilots in an experimental aircraft that goes Mach 10 but vibrates so hard our teeth hurt.

Until we figure out the balance, the fatigue will remain. The paradox will persist. We will keep shipping record-breaking amounts of software while feeling like we haven’t actually *built* anything in months. We will keep staring at that blinking cursor, waiting for the digital ghost to tell us what to do next.

So the next time you feel that heavy, gray exhaustion settling in after a day of “productivity,” know that you are not alone. It is not just you. It is the friction of the future rubbing against the limitations of the biology we inherited from our ancestors.

And if you really need a break, you can always try that workout plan. I hear the “waiting for the reasoning model to finish thinking” plank challenge is a real killer for the core. Just don’t ask the AI to count the seconds for you. It might hallucinate that a minute only has forty seconds, and honestly, we could all use a little bit of that kind of error right now.

The solution isn’t to stop using the tools. We can’t go back. The genie is out of the bottle, and he is writing really fast Python. The solution is to reclaim our agency. We need to remember that we are the engineers. The AI is the tool. We decide when to pick it up, and more importantly, we decide when to put it down.

We need to be okay with shipping a little less code if it means understanding that code a little more. We need to be okay with taking an hour to solve a problem the “hard way” just to prove to ourselves that we still can. We need to step off the assembly line occasionally and remember what it feels like to craft something with our own hands, even if it is just a terrible shell script that we wrote without a single suggestion from the cloud.

So take a breath. Close the tab. Go for a walk. The code will still be there when you get back. And if it isn’t, well, the AI probably overwrote it with something better anyway. Just kidding. It definitely introduced a bug. You better check that.
