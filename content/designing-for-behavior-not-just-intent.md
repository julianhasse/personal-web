#### The Reality Gap

In healthcare, this gap between design intent and real-world use is not just aesthetic—it’s operational, ethical, and sometimes life-altering.

Clinicians don’t always use EHR systems as intended. They copy-paste notes to save time. They memorize shortcuts to bypass slow forms. They repurpose fields meant for one purpose to store data for another. Not because they’re careless—but because the system wasn’t designed around their reality.

Patients do it too. They use fitness trackers not to monitor steps, but as accountability partners. They open telehealth apps just to message clinicians with questions they don’t want to ask in person. They photograph medication labels to remember dosages instead of using built-in “reminder” features.
Every improvised action is a clue. Every misuse is a design insight in disguise.

---

#### Design Intent vs. Human Behavior

We design for ideal users—the ones who follow the flow we imagine.
But healthcare is messy. It’s emotional, high-stakes, and full of edge cases.

No designer can fully predict what a nurse under pressure will do when the EHR lags during an emergency, or how a patient with limited literacy will navigate a digital consent form.
People adapt faster than systems. They find their own shortcuts. They make the interface work *for them*, not the other way around.

When we resist this, we design against reality.
When we embrace it, we unlock innovation.

---

#### The Hidden Value of Improvisation

In healthcare, some of the most valuable features have emerged from unintended use.

- **The “note-to-self” field** that became a collaborative space for multidisciplinary teams.
- **The secure messaging tool** that evolved into a coordination hub for discharge planning.
- **The appointment scheduler** repurposed by patients as a way to track symptoms.

These aren’t design failures—they’re design evolutions.
They reveal what people *really* need when the system stops getting in their way.

Observing these patterns doesn’t mean surrendering control; it means recognizing that value often hides in behavior, not intent.

---

#### Designing for Emergence

The question for healthcare designers isn’t just, “Does this meet requirements?”
It’s, “What happens when someone uses this differently than I expect?”

Can the system bend without breaking?
Can it adapt without confusing?
Can it grow from how people actually behave?

Adaptive design in healthcare means building products that *listen*—to user behavior, to context, to improvisation. It means seeing “misuse” as a form of user creativity, not a flaw.

The goal isn’t to constrain behavior but to understand it deeply enough to guide it safely and productively.

---

#### Behavior as a Catalyst for Growth

When we pay attention to these hidden behaviors, we find unexpected opportunities:
A neglected feature becomes a bridge to a new workflow.
An off-label use becomes a prototype for the next release.
A workaround becomes a roadmap.

Just as medicine evolves by observing patient response, healthcare design should evolve by observing user response.
That’s how innovation moves from theory to impact—from intent to lived experience.

---

#### The Provocation

Are you designing for intent or for behavior?

Every product has a hidden life after launch—a parallel reality shaped by the people who use it under pressure, in context, and often in contradiction to the original brief.

The most transformative healthcare solutions don’t come from enforcing compliance.
They come from embracing reality.
From watching, learning, and evolving with the user.

Because somewhere in that messy, improvised behavior might be the spark that changes everything.
