*A response to Chris Best’s latest vision for the future of writing.*

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/011aa517-579d-4391-8395-cddb1de57a5f_1536x1024.png)

For years I believed Substack represented something rare on the internet.

A place where creators owned their audience.
A place that rewarded ideas instead of algorithms.
A place that trusted writers.

This week, I realized that vision is changing.

Substack has begun rolling out AI detection tools, allowing readers to scan posts and estimate how much was written by a human versus AI. The company says the goal is transparency, not punishment, and CEO Chris Best insists the problem isn’t AI itself, but the loss of trust that comes when readers don’t know how something was created. ([The Verge](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/968855/substack-pangram-ai-detecting-tool?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

I believe this is a profound mistake.

Not because AI detectors are inaccurate—although many are. Not because they produce false positives—although they absolutely do.
But because they begin from the wrong premise.

The premise is that *how* something is created matters more than *what* it creates.

I reject that completely.

## This Article Was Written With AI

Let’s get the confession out of the way.

Yes.

I wrote this with AI.
Not because I couldn’t write it myself.
Because I chose to.

AI helped me organize arguments, challenge weak points, tighten paragraphs, and explore alternative ways to communicate ideas.

Exactly like:

- an editor
- a research assistant
- a thesaurus
- a grammar checker
- Google
- Stack Overflow
- spell check

Should every sentence receive a forensic analysis because I used a better tool?

Should Photoshop users disclose every layer?
Should musicians disclose every compressor plugin?
Should Substack developers label every line touched by GitHub Copilot?
At what point does using software become cheating?

## The Calculator Moment

History repeats itself.

People once argued calculators would destroy mathematics.
Word processors would destroy writing.
Photography would destroy painting.
Synthesizers would destroy music.
Digital cameras would destroy professional photography.
Every generation mistakes a new tool for the death of a craft.

The craft survives.

The mediocre adapt poorly.
The exceptional adapt brilliantly.
AI belongs in that same lineage.

## AI Doesn’t Replace Thinking

Bad writers produce bad writing.
Now they simply do it faster.
Great writers produce better writing.
Now they explore more possibilities.
The intelligence never came from the keyboard.
It came from the human asking questions.
AI does not manufacture taste.
It doesn’t invent curiosity.
It doesn’t replace judgment.

Those remain uniquely human.

Ironically, they are precisely the qualities that separate excellent AI-assisted work from mediocre AI-assisted work.

## We’re Measuring the Wrong Thing

Imagine walking into an art museum.

Instead of asking,

> Is this beautiful?

you ask,

> Was Photoshop used?

Imagine judging a movie based on whether CGI appears in one scene.
Imagine reviewing a novel based on whether Grammarly corrected commas.
This is where AI detection leads.
Readers become detectives.
Creators become defendants.
Discussion shifts away from ideas and toward production methods.
That is a loss for everyone.

## AI Is Becoming Literacy

In ten years we’ll stop saying:

> “This was written with AI.”

For the same reason we don’t say:

> “This article was typed on a laptop.”

Or:

> “This book was edited using Microsoft Word.”

The tool disappears.

The work remains.

Future generations will consider AI-assisted writing as ordinary as spell check.

Trying to distinguish “AI writing” from “human writing” will feel increasingly artificial because nearly everyone will use AI in some part of their creative process.

The meaningful distinction won’t be AI versus no AI.

It will be thoughtful versus thoughtless.

## Trust Doesn’t Come From Detection

Chris Best argues that readers deserve transparency.

On that, I agree.
But transparency should be voluntary.
Not algorithmically inferred.
Readers build trust over months and years.
By reading.
By observing.
By seeing whether a creator consistently produces insightful work.
Trust is earned through ideas.

Not through detector scores.

## The Irony

Substack built its reputation by believing creators know best.
Now it is asking algorithms to evaluate creators.
That feels backwards.
Creators should decide how they work.
Readers should decide what they value.
Platforms should facilitate that relationship—not police it.

## Why I’m Leaving

I’m not leaving because of AI detection itself.
I’m leaving because of the philosophy behind it.

A philosophy that quietly implies AI-assisted creativity is somehow less authentic.

I don’t believe that.
I believe we’re entering the most exciting era in the history of human creativity.
An era where one person can research like a team.

Write like a newsroom.
Design like a studio.
Code like a startup.
Translate instantly.
Compose music.
Prototype ideas in minutes.
None of that diminishes human creativity.

It amplifies it.

The future belongs to creators who know how to think.
AI simply gives them a louder voice.

So yes.

I wrote this with AI.
And I hope more creators do too.
Not because AI makes us less human.
But because it lets us spend less time fighting our tools—and more time expressing what only humans can imagine.
