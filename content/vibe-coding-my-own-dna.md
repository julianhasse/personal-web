I recently inherited a massive legacy codebase. It is poorly documented, full of spaghetti logic, and the original developer has been ghosting everyone for about four billion years. There are no comments, the variable names are incomprehensible, and if I try to refactor the wrong line, the whole system crashes permanently.

I am talking about my DNA.

As a designer and software engineer working in the healthcare industry, I spend my days staring at Electronic Health Records. They are clunky, opaque interfaces designed by committees who seemingly hate human beings. So when I got my raw DNA data back from an ancestry test, I did not want to upload it to yet another “health insights” platform that charges a subscription just to tell me I have green eyes.

I decided to treat my genome like what it actually is. It is just a text file. It is 15 megabytes of As, Gs, Cs, and Ts. And because it is text, that means it is code. And because it is code, that means I can vibe code it.

I opened my AI code editor. This is usually where I build UI prototypes, but today I dragged in a file named `Julian_Hasse_v5_Full_20260101201559.txt`.

The AI paused. It is used to seeing JavaScript loops, not the raw source code of a human being. I typed a prompt that would make a geneticist faint. I told it to act like a Senior DevOps Engineer looking at a server log that is throwing warnings. I wanted to grep my own existence.

The file contains seven hundred thousand lines of Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms, or SNPs. In developer terms, think of a SNP as a feature flag in your body’s `config.json` file. One setting determines if you can digest milk. Another determines if you panic when you drink coffee.

The AI immediately created a todo list. This is where the healthcare designer in me gets giddy. If I went to a doctor and asked them to check my pharmacogenomic compatibility or my methylation pathway, it would take weeks of referrals and insurance battles. Here? The AI just started writing `grep` commands.

If you are not technical, `grep` is a command-line tool used to search for text. Seeing it used on my biology was a spiritual experience.

It ran a command searching for `rs429358`. To the uninitiated, that looks like a random serial number. To longevity nerds, that is the APOE gene, the primary risk factor for Alzheimer’s. My heart rate spiked as I watched the terminal output. This was not a soft, comforting UI with a green gauge. This was bare metal biology. I was debugging my brain’s future firmware.

This is the essence of vibe coding. I did not write a Python script. I did not import `pandas`. I just vibed. I told the computer what I was curious about, and it wrote the regex to find the answer.

We moved on to pharmacogenomics. This describes how your liver handles drugs. The AI grepped for `CYP2C19`, a gene that metabolizes antidepressants and blood thinners. It cross-referenced my genotype with medical literature and told me which drugs my system treats like water and which ones it treats like poison. I sat back and stared at the screen. I just saved myself years of potential trial-and-error, all because I treated my body like a software project.

There is a subversive power in this. The healthcare industry thrives on keeping data locked in massive systems. When you download your raw DNA, you are circumventing the institution. You are taking the source code home. But for a long time, that file was useless noise. You needed a specialized interpreter.

AI is that interpreter. It is the perfect pair programmer. It does not judge you for asking about male pattern baldness risks immediately after asking about heart disease. It just runs the search.

Of course, there are risks. The AI can hallucinate. It is not a doctor. Please do not perform surgery on yourself based on a chat session. But let us be real. The standard of care is a fifteen-minute slot once a year. If I can use AI to flag a potential issue that I can then bring up to a professional, I am already miles ahead.

I closed the editor. I did not commit any code. You cannot really push updates to your DNA yet since CRISPR is still in beta and the deployment pipeline is a regulatory nightmare. But I felt different.

I felt like I had finally read the documentation.

For years, I have been operating this machine by pressing buttons and seeing what happens. Now I have seen the schematic. I know where the weak solder joints are. I know which capacitors are likely to blow if I over-volt them.

So if you have your raw DNA data sitting in a zip file, drag it into your code editor. Ask an AI to take a look. You might find out that you are not broken. You just have some weird legacy dependencies that haven’t been updated since the Stone Age.

And knowing that? That is half the battle. Good luck explaining to your doctor that you found a null pointer exception in your vitamin D receptor, though. I will let you know how that conversation goes.
