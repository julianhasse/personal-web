#### Summary

A recent federal court ruling against the FDA’s authority to regulate laboratory-developed tests (LDTs) could destabilize laboratory standards, jeopardize patient safety, and weaken the reliability of EHR-driven diagnostics. This decision, hinging on the distinction between tangible devices and intangible services, sends ripple effects across laboratory medicine and electronic health record (EHR) infrastructure. With federal oversight in question, the path forward for integrating high-risk tests into clinical workflows becomes fragmented, legally ambiguous, and technically inconsistent.

The reliability of modern diagnostics depends not only on scientific rigor, but on **regulatory structure**. In 2024, the FDA aimed to address long-standing concerns about the uneven regulation of **laboratory-developed tests (LDTs)**—tests designed, manufactured, and used within a single lab—by issuing a rule to bring them under its device regulatory framework. The intention was clear: protect patients from potentially misleading results, as in the case of invalid ovarian and breast cancer tests that influenced harmful treatment decisions.

But in March 2025, a Texas federal judge vacated the rule, claiming that LDTs do not meet the FDCA’s definition of a device. Instead, he argued, these tests are intangible services. If this interpretation holds, it could dismantle years of progress in ensuring clinical testing accuracy and consistency.

The implications for **laboratories** are immediate and structural. Without FDA oversight, labs revert to regulation solely under the **Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)**, which govern analytical accuracy but **not clinical validity**. That gap—between detecting a biomarker and interpreting its meaning—leaves a blind spot in patient care. State-level regulation, while possible, lacks the FDA’s consistency and scope, creating a **patchwork system** of oversight.

For **EHR systems**, the decision also has deep ramifications. Clinical decision support tools increasingly rely on structured, validated input from lab tests. When LDTs feed into EHRs, particularly in oncology, infectious diseases, and genomics, the **validity of these inputs becomes critical**. Without FDA-enforced standards, the risk of flawed test data entering diagnostic algorithms rises, potentially compromising the outputs that physicians rely on.

Moreover, the ruling could erode the FDA’s ability to regulate **emerging digital diagnostics**, including software and AI tools integrated into lab workflows and EHRs. The court’s logic—that intangible services are outside FDA jurisdiction—could be cited in future challenges to oversight of algorithmic diagnostic support or clinical-grade software.

As federal authority weakens, **liability shifts downstream**. Labs may face lawsuits from harmed patients, and hospitals could see EHR vendors pull back from integrating high-risk diagnostics. Compliance costs could increase, and innovation may slow in the absence of clear, national guidelines.

This ruling also intersects with broader trends in administrative law. The **Loper Bright decision** curtailed deference to agencies’ interpretations of ambiguous statutes. If courts continue to strip away interpretive authority from experts like the FDA, the entire ecosystem—from labs to EHR developers—will operate in a less predictable regulatory environment.

---

### Conclusion

The invalidation of the FDA’s LDT rule is more than a legal technicality—it’s a turning point for the integration of diagnostics, software, and clinical practice. Without federal standards, labs and EHR systems are left navigating a fractured landscape where risk, reliability, and responsibility are diffused. Whether through appeal, legislation, or a regulatory reset, restoring coherence and confidence in diagnostic oversight is not just a matter of policy—it’s a matter of public health.
