Before we dive into the types of research questions, it’s worth asking: **What makes a research question useful in the first place?** In clinical data research, a useful question tends to have a few key properties:

- **It addresses a real need.** The question should connect to decisions clinicians, researchers, or healthcare systems actually need to make.
- **It can be answered with the data we have (or can reasonably collect).** No matter how interesting a question is, it’s not useful if we have no way to answer it.
- **It’s clear and focused.** A vague or overly broad question is hard to answer well — and hard to act on even if you do.
- **It offers actionable insights.** Ideally, the answer helps guide decisions, whether it’s improving patient care, adjusting treatment plans, or understanding disease better.

With those properties in mind, we can break down **the different types of questions you might ask when working with clinical data.**

### 1. Descriptive Questions

These are the simplest type — they ask for a summary of the data itself. For example, **what proportion of patients in our dataset have familial hypercholesterolemia?** Descriptive questions focus on counts, averages, or proportions, giving us a basic snapshot of the population.

### 2. Exploratory Questions

Exploratory questions dive deeper, trying to **find patterns within the data.** A good example would be: **What subtypes of autism exist within patients at our medical center?** This kind of question might involve techniques like clustering, where we group patients based on different characteristics.

### 3. Inferential Questions

Inferential questions aim to **find patterns that extend beyond the specific data at hand.** For instance: **Is there a link between gut bacteria and depression?** These patterns might suggest associations, but they don’t necessarily tell us the cause.

### 4. Predictive Questions

These questions are all about **forecasting outcomes.** For example: **Which patients are likely to need high-cost care next year?** Predictions rely on patterns we’ve found in the data, but they don’t explain why those patterns exist.

### 5. Causal Questions

Causal questions ask about **the effect of one change on another.** For example: **For a patient with hypertension and asthma whose type 2 diabetes isn’t controlled with Metformin, what’s the best second-line treatment?** This type of question tries to understand what action will directly improve outcomes.

### 6. Deterministic Questions

Finally, deterministic questions go even deeper — they ask about **the actual mechanisms behind a relationship.** For example: **How does androgen deprivation increase the risk of dementia?** These questions aim to uncover the biological or physiological pathways driving a pattern.

---

**Why This Matters**
Framing your research question the right way — and understanding what type of question you’re asking — helps guide the methods you use, the data you need, and what kind of conclusions you can draw. **Whether you’re describing, exploring, predicting, or digging into causality, choosing the right approach makes all the difference.**
