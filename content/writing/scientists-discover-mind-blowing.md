## **How Human Vision Works (and Where It Breaks)**

Before we can grasp what "olo" is, we need to understand how normal color perception works.

Human eyes rely on three types of cone cells—S (short), M (medium), and L (long)—which roughly correspond to blue, green, and red wavelengths. These cones overlap in their sensitivity, and our brain combines their signals to create the full spectrum of color we perceive.

But there are hard limits:

- We cannot see ultraviolet or infrared without technological aid.
- Colors exist only in *contrast*—our brain calculates "color" by comparing signals from multiple cones.

This means that many colors (like true “red-green,” called a “forbidden color”) are theoretically impossible to see—unless you trick the brain.

## **The Experiment: Zapping M Cones**

Here’s where the mad science kicks in.

The researchers at Berkeley used a technique called **two-photon chromatic stimulation**, which involves delivering pulses of laser light at a cellular level to selectively activate **M cones** (the green-sensitive ones), without affecting the others. It’s a bit like playing a single string on a guitar without making the others vibrate.

### The Setup:

- A precise laser delivery system targets a single photoreceptor.
- The subject stares into the device and signals what they perceive.
- What they describe is… uncharted territory.

They report something they *know* to be a color, but:

- It doesn’t match any color on the visible spectrum.
- It isn’t a combination of existing colors.
- It’s internally consistent, vivid, and impossible to replicate on screen.

## **Describing the Indescribable: What Is “Olo”?**

How do you describe a color you’ve never seen? You don’t.

But participants tried:

- “It was like a deep emerald but neonized.”
- “It felt real, not imagined, and I can't compare it to anything I've seen before.”
- “It wasn’t green or blue or turquoise—it was its own thing.”

The team coined the name **“olo”** as a placeholder—short, round, strange. Just like the color.

The name mimics how we invent words for abstract feelings—onomatopoeic and playful, like “quark” or “blip.” It’s meaningless, yet perfect.

## 5. **The Neuroscience of Synthetic Color**

What’s happening in the brain when someone sees “olo”? The M cone is triggered in isolation, and the brain—lacking signals from the other cones—creates a compensatory hallucination. It’s not unlike phantom limb syndrome, where the brain invents sensations based on incomplete input.

But here’s the kicker: This is not a *malfunction*—it’s a perfectly constructed sensory response, generated by your brain in real-time. That’s what makes it a true “new” color experience.

This research intersects with concepts in **neuroplasticity** and **sensory substitution**:

- Can we train ourselves to perceive more colors?
- Could people with color blindness be given access to olo?

## **Philosophical Implications – Is Reality Just What We Perceive?**

Color is not a property of the world—it’s a construct of the brain. Olo highlights how limited our senses are and how much reality we *don’t* perceive.

This isn’t new to philosophers:

- Kant argued we never perceive the “thing-in-itself”—only its appearance.
- Plato’s Allegory of the Cave suggests our reality is just shadows of a deeper truth.

Olo is a shadow... but a very colorful one.

## **Limitations and Ethical Considerations**

Of course, there are some caveats:

- **The color isn’t stable.** It fades quickly and must be re-triggered.
- **There’s no record.** You can’t photograph olo or print it in a journal.
- **The experiment is invasive.** It involves precise eye targeting—risky, expensive, and not widely accessible.

And then there’s the ethics:

- What happens when we can *customize* perception?
- Will “enhanced perception” become a class privilege?
- Could it alter how we trust our senses—or each other?

This is not just a science problem—it’s a humanity problem.
