A few days ago I published an article called **“Why I’m Leaving Substack (Yes, I Wrote This with AI).**”

At the time, I thought I was leaving because of a philosophical disagreement. Substack had started treating AI-assisted writing as something suspicious, something to detect, discourage, and eventually regulate. As someone who believes AI is simply another creative tool, I couldn’t support that direction.

But after spending the last week rebuilding my publishing platform from scratch, I realized that wasn’t the real story.

The real story is ownership.

## I didn’t replace Substack.
I replaced the idea that I needed it.

With a lot of help from AI, I rebuilt my entire publishing workflow around technologies that have existed for decades: HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Markdown, and Git.

No CMS.
No database.
No plugins.
No vendor lock-in.

Just files.

It turns out that’s incredibly liberating.

## Every article is just a Markdown file
One of the biggest surprises was how simple everything became.

To publish a new article, I create a Markdown file inside a folder:

content/
    writing/
        my-new-article.md
That’s it.

The website reads it automatically and renders it as a fully formatted article.

Images? They’re just files in an assets folder.
Projects? Same idea.
There’s no editor to log into.
No dashboard to learn.
No proprietary format.
My writing is now plain text.

If a better platform appears five years from now, my content is already portable.

## My website became my CMS
This is probably my favorite part.

People often assume a Content Management System has to be WordPress, Ghost, or something equally complex.

But what is a CMS, really?
It’s simply a way to organize and publish content.
My website now does exactly that.
Projects live in one folder. Articles live in another.
Each piece has metadata in its frontmatter.
The homepage updates automatically.
The project list updates automatically.
The article pages are generated automatically.
The entire “CMS” is a collection of folders that I control.

There’s something wonderfully satisfying about that.

## I own the technology
When you publish on someone else’s platform, you’re renting space.
You don’t notice until the landlord changes the rules.
Maybe AI-generated content becomes restricted.
Maybe membership policies change.
Maybe algorithms change. Maybe pricing changes.
Maybe the company disappears.

Your content may still be yours legally, but operationally you’re dependent on someone else’s decisions.

Today, my publishing stack consists of technologies that will almost certainly outlive any startup.

HTML.
Markdown.
Git.

Those aren’t trends.
They’re infrastructure.

## AI became part of the publishing workflow
The irony isn’t lost on me.

**I left a platform because of its attitude toward AI...**

**...and then used AI to build the replacement.**

Not only did AI help me design the website, it also helped me:

design the information architecture

build the article renderer

create a lightweight project portfolio

migrate my Substack archive

convert every exported article into Markdown

improve typography and layout

simplify the publishing workflow

think about future features like search, RSS, newsletters, and analytics

AI didn’t replace the work.

It accelerated it.
It became a collaborator.

Exactly as I believe these tools should.

## This is only the beginning
Ironically, by removing a publishing platform, I’ve gained more possibilities.

Because I own the code, I can add features whenever I want.
An AI-powered search assistant. Automatic article recommendations.
Interactive demonstrations. Podcast transcripts.
Knowledge graphs.
A personal research assistant trained on everything I’ve written.
None of those ideas require waiting for a company roadmap.

If I can imagine it, I can build it.

## The lesson wasn’t about Substack
Substack was never really the problem.

It simply reminded me of something I already knew.

**Whenever we build our work on someone else’s platform, we’re accepting someone else’s constraints.**

Sometimes that’s perfectly reasonable.
Sometimes it isn’t.
This time, I decided to take ownership instead.
I still believe newsletters are valuable.
I still believe independent publishing matters.
But I’ve come to believe something else as well.
The most valuable platform you can build is the one you control.
My articles are mine.
My domain is mine.
My technology stack is mine.
My future is no longer tied to a single company’s policies.

For a designer who has spent a career helping other people build products, there’s something deeply satisfying about finally building a publishing platform that feels like my own.

And the best part?

This isn’t the end of the journey.

It’s version 1.0.
