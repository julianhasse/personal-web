## Summary

While the tech world rides the rocket ship of Moore's Law into hyperspace, pharmaceutical development chugs along in a horse-drawn cart named **Eroom's Law**. Drug discovery, unlike AI or microchips, has stubbornly resisted exponential efficiency. In fact, it’s getting slower and more expensive. Despite the promises of AI-powered miracles, the cost of developing new drugs continues to double approximately every nine years. This article explores the causes of Eroom’s Law, how AI is both the hero and the court jester trying to fix it, and why pharma innovation remains one of the most complex—and comically frustrating—challenges of the 21st century.

# **Eroom's Law — Progress, But in Reverse**

Let's set the stage:
Moore’s Law says that every two years, computers get roughly twice as powerful at the same price. Cue fireworks. Cue startup founders popping champagne on LinkedIn.

Now enter **Eroom's Law**—yes, Moore spelled backward, and for good reason.
It describes the infuriating reality that **the cost of developing a new drug doubles about every nine years**. Progress is not exponential. It's... well, *negative exponential*. If Moore’s Law is a high-speed train, Eroom’s Law is a guy trying to jog backwards through molasses while carrying a piano.

First coined by Jack Scannell and colleagues in 2012, Eroom’s Law reflects a truth pharmaceutical companies know all too well: despite fancier tools, better computers, and hotter buzzwords, **drug development is slower, riskier, and more expensive than ever**.

In short: if you think your AI model training times are slow, be glad you’re not working on a new arthritis drug.

# **Why Is Drug Discovery So Painfully Slow?**

Several culprits have been fingered in the great whodunnit of pharma failure:

### 1. **The Better Than The Beatles Problem**

Once upon a time, The Beatles dominated pop charts. Today, if you want a hit song, it has to be better than The Beatles—and also compete with Spotify algorithms, TikTok dances, and every indie genius on Bandcamp.

Same goes for drugs: **the low-hanging fruit was picked decades ago**. New drugs must outperform existing ones that already work quite well. Good luck impressing the FDA with your slightly improved blood pressure pill when there’s 37 others.

### 2. **The Cautious Regulator Effect**

You think your code has to pass QA? Try getting FDA approval.
Agencies demand near-perfect data for safety and efficacy—and rightly so.
But this means **trials are getting longer, larger, and crazier expensive**. It's not unusual for Phase III trials to include tens of thousands of participants and cost upwards of $500 million... just for a *maybe*.

### 3. **The Throw More Bodies at It Fallacy**

Adding more researchers doesn't scale like adding more GPUs.
More people means more meetings, more documentation, and more versions of the same PowerPoint deck.
Pharma R&D teams have ballooned in size but haven't necessarily gotten faster—or better.

### 4. **The Complexity Monster**

Biology isn’t software.
Proteins fold weirdly. Cells behave unpredictably. Human bodies are black boxes with moods.
You can’t just patch a bug in the "immune system v2.7" and push an update overnight.

# **Enter AI — Savior or Sidekick?**

Now, if you’ve been to any biotech conference in the past five years, you’ve seen it:
Every booth promises "AI-powered drug discovery," "machine learning-enabled molecule generation," or "neural networks that whisper to proteins."

Cool.

**But does AI actually kill Eroom’s Law?**

The truth: **AI is helping—but slowly, cautiously, and comically imperfectly**.

Let's explore:

### 1. **AI Accelerates the Easy Parts**

- **Molecular modeling:** AI can predict how molecules might bind to proteins faster than any human.
- **Target discovery:** Machine learning finds promising biological pathways with massive data mining.
- **Virtual screening:** Billions of compounds can be simulated in silico, narrowing candidates for real-world testing.

This saves months—sometimes years—in the early "guess and check" stages.

### 2. **AI Struggles with the Hard Parts**

- **Clinical trials:** AI can't yet predict human side effects, trial logistics, or regulatory hurdles.
- **Long-term efficacy:** No model can truly simulate the messy, hormonal, occasionally pizza-fueled chaos that is human biology over decades.
- **Behavioral compliance:** AI can invent the best treatment in the world... but if patients won't take their meds properly, it’s useless.

### 3. **AI and "Data Garbage In, Garbage Out"**

Early-stage drug data is often messy, sparse, biased, and full of sampling errors.
Feeding this into a fancy deep learning model?
It's like teaching a self-driving car with footage from bumper cars at a carnival.

# **Pharma Case Studies — When AI Actually Works**

### Case 1: BenevolentAI and COVID-19

In early 2020, AI models helped identify baricitinib, an existing rheumatoid arthritis drug, as a potential COVID-19 treatment.
It moved into clinical trials at record speed, eventually earning emergency use authorization.
**AI shaved months off discovery time**, possibly saving lives.

### Case 2: Insilico Medicine

Insilico used generative adversarial networks (GANs) to design novel molecules in record time.
One promising fibrosis drug went from target discovery to preclinical trials in under 18 months—a timeline unheard of without AI.

### Case 3: Atomwise

Atomwise uses deep learning to predict molecule-protein interactions.
Their platform has initiated over 750 partnerships with universities and pharma companies to identify drug candidates more efficiently.

# **What This Means for Healthcare and Patients**

### 1. **Hope for Rare Diseases**

AI could finally make it economically feasible to develop treatments for rare ("orphan") diseases.
Smaller patient populations are no longer a deal-breaker when you can streamline research with AI.

### 2. **Personalized Medicine**

AI makes it possible to design therapies tailored to individual genomes, lifestyles, and medical histories.
Goodbye "one-size-fits-all" pills, hello bespoke biotech.

### 3. **Faster Emergency Responses**

In future pandemics, AI could reduce the time from sequence discovery to vaccine development by months—maybe weeks.

### 4. **Cheaper Drugs... Maybe**

In theory, more efficient discovery should reduce costs.
In reality, companies like profits.
Expect drug prices to fall roughly two weeks after the Heat Death of the Universe.

---

# **Conclusion: Eroom's Law vs. The March of AI**

Eroom's Law reminds us that **not every industry bends easily to Moore-style exponential magic**.
Biology is messy. Clinical trials are brutal. Regulators are cautious.
And sometimes, **a human cell just doesn’t give a damn how many teraflops your AI model has**.

Yet, AI is undeniably changing the game—making early-stage drug discovery faster, smarter, and occasionally, cheaper.
It's the jolt pharma needed: not a revolution, but a caffeinated jog toward efficiency.

**In pharma, progress might be slow—but with AI brewing the coffee, we’re finally starting to move.**
