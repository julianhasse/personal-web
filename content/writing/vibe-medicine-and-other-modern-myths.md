There is a trend floating around design circles where people are calling their use of Lovable or Figma Make “vibe coding.” I saw this phrase and felt the kind of instinctive jolt that developers feel when someone says they “just pushed to prod” from a coffee shop on hotel Wi-Fi. It is the type of phrase that sounds fun and harmless until you realize it is quietly rewriting the laws of software physics.

Before the term spreads any further, let us clear something up. Lovable and Figma Make are not vibe coding. Not even close. And vibe coding itself is not how real applications get built. If anything, both terms are useful metaphors for a deeper point that is becoming more important by the day, especially when we shift the analogy into healthcare. Because just like you cannot vibe code your way into a secure, scalable product, you also cannot vibe your way into medical diagnosis. We will come back to that.

Vibe coding, as described by Andrej Karpathy, is that exploratory, lightly structured mode where you chat with an AI to generate quick scripts for fun ideas or little experiments. You throw prompts at the machine. The machine throws code back. You poke at it. It kind of works. Sometimes it works too well and makes you suspicious of what it is not showing you. It is a playful feedback loop between human curiosity and machine pattern generation. Great for prototypes. Great for imagination. Horrible for anything intended to survive users who click the wrong thing on purpose.

This is why vibe coding is not engineering. Engineers do not build production systems by just asking the AI, “Can you make me a full stack web app for dogs who want their own crypto tokens?” and then hitting deploy. They rely on architecture, data modeling, testing, security, access control, observability, performance thinking, and the knowledge that if something breaks in production at 3 a.m., they will be the ones who wake up to fix it. Vibe coding does not carry the burden of consequences, and therefore it cannot be responsible for reliability.

Lovable and Figma Make are even further away from real coding. They are more like vibe prototyping. You describe what you want, the tool spins something up, and suddenly you have a clickable experience that looks shockingly presentable for something that required no code. It is amazing for designers because it collapses the time between idea and prototype. You can show stakeholders a living thing, not a static mock. It is genuinely transformative for early exploration.

But calling that vibe coding is like calling a vending machine a chef. No one is doing engineering here. There is no error handling. No system design. No consideration for memory usage or scaling. No real testing other than “Does this click feel good?” Designers should absolutely use these tools. They are brilliant additions to the workflow. They free up developers from prototype churn. They encourage creativity and reduce bottlenecks. But they should not give anyone the illusion that prototypes equal products. That illusion is already widespread enough.

Now comes the part where the analogy becomes very interesting. Because the same vibe energy that people apply to coding is creeping into discussions around medical AI. This is where the stakes get much higher. If vibe coding is the experimental playground for quick scripts, vibe medicine is the equivalent for medical suggestions from AI models. And while vibe coding can crash your app, vibe medicine can produce false confidence that affects real health outcomes.

Vibe medicine happens when someone pastes their symptoms into an AI chatbot and gets a long list of possible diagnoses. The model is remarkably capable at summarizing patterns. It can explain possible causes for chest pain or headaches or joint issues. It can synthesize medical papers into simple language. It can draft differentials that resemble the structure used by clinicians. The problem is that it does all this without physical exams, without lab results, without imaging, without longitudinal context, without liability, and without the actual clinical intuition humans rely on in high stakes situations.

AI can describe patterns. It can recognize probabilities. It can quote medical literature with suspicious elegance. But real medicine involves the messy, uncertain, intuitive work of determining whether a patient is actually ok or whether something subtle is hiding underneath the surface. A model can say “This looks like anemia,” but a clinician says “Your hemoglobin is normal, your presentation does not match the pattern, and you seem more fatigued than you admit.” A model can suggest pneumonia based on symptoms, but a doctor can look at an X ray and notice that the opacity is not in the right location, or that the patient’s oxygen saturation does not align with the severity the model implies.

That is the difference between vibe medicine and real medicine. Vibe medicine produces ideas. Medicine produces decisions. The former is risk free narration. The latter carries responsibility. A medical professional cannot shrug and say “Well, the model said so.” A medical professional cannot ignore contraindications or drug interactions because the model hallucinated confidence. A medical professional cannot treat symptoms without considering the patient as a whole. AI does not yet understand physiology, pathology, or human variability at a mechanistic level. It predicts. It does not reason.

This is why the AI healthcare conversation keeps returning to the phrase “human in the loop.” Not because it sounds cool, but because it reflects the difference between inspiration and care. An AI can support diagnosis, but it cannot own it. It can surface insights, but it cannot integrate them into clinical judgment. It can reduce cognitive load, but it cannot replace licensed accountability. In vibe coding, if the AI generates something wrong, you debug it. In vibe medicine, if the AI generates something wrong, the stakes are far greater and someone must catch it.

This is not to say clinicians should reject AI. Far from it. The same way that engineers benefit from AI assisted coding, clinicians stand to benefit from AI assisted diagnostics. A model that suggests rare conditions that a doctor might not consider can be useful. A tool that reads imaging with precision can be revolutionary. A summarizer that extracts key details from a complex chart can save time. But these tools need skilled humans to interpret them, question them, override them, and decide how to act. The human is not the fallback. The human is the operator.

Vibes are powerful. They spark ideas. They unlock creativity. They help you explore the space of possibilities. But vibes cannot anchor a product. And vibes cannot anchor a diagnosis. For that, you need expertise. You need rigor. You need professionals. You need adults in the room.

Use the tools. Use the AI. Use the vibes. But respect the depth.
