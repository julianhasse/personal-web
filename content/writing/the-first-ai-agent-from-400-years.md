Long before we had chatbots, copilots, autonomous workflows, robotic process automation, or AI agents quietly clicking through tasks on our behalf, Jewish folklore imagined something strangely familiar: a human-made servant, built from inert material, activated by language, given a job, and eventually feared by the person who created it.

That creature was the golem.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/2e095974-14a5-4745-9f16-4cf18eb667e7_1122x1402.png)

The golem is usually described as a figure made from clay, mud, lime, or other lifeless matter, brought to life through mystical knowledge. In Jewish tradition, the idea appears in several forms, but the most famous versions involve rabbis and kabbalists who animate a humanoid servant or protector through sacred letters, divine names, or inscriptions. The best-known golem story is often associated with Rabbi Judah Loew ben Bezalel, the Maharal of Prague, in the late sixteenth century. But an older tradition connects the creation of a golem to Rabbi Eliyahu Baal Shem of Chełm, a Polish rabbi and kabbalist who lived in the sixteenth century and was later remembered as one of the earliest figures associated with this kind of artificial life.

If we look at the golem through modern eyes, it begins to resemble an early myth of automation. Not because it was “technology” in the mechanical sense, but because it captured the same anxieties that surround technology today: What happens when humans create something that can act in the world? What happens when a tool becomes an agent? What happens when obedience is not the same as understanding?

The golem may be the first AI agent from 400 years ago.

Not literally, of course. It was not built with neural networks, trained on data, or connected to APIs. But as an allegory, it is almost uncomfortably modern. It is an artificial worker. It is activated by symbolic instructions. It performs tasks. It scales beyond expectation. It lacks judgment. And when its creator loses control, the very system designed to reduce labor becomes a source of danger.

According to the tale, there was once a Jew in Poland named Rabbi Eliyahu Baal Shem who made a golem from lime to serve him and do housework. The golem was animated by having the word *emet*, meaning “truth,” written on its forehead. But the servant did not remain modest or manageable. It kept growing. It became stronger and larger, until its size itself became a threat.

To stop it, the rabbi needed to erase the first letter, the *alef*, from *emet*. Without the *alef*, the word became *met*, meaning “dead.” This small deletion would deactivate the creature. The logic is elegant and terrifying: life and death separated by one letter, agency controlled by a symbolic switch.

In the Polish story, the rabbi faces a practical problem. The golem has grown so large that he can no longer reach its forehead. The control interface is now physically out of reach. So he devises a trick. He commands the golem to take off its boots, assuming that when it bends down, he will be able to erase the letter from its forehead. The plan works, but only partly. The golem bends. The rabbi erases the letter. The servant turns back into clay. Then the entire weight of the collapsing body falls on the rabbi and crushes him.

It is hard to imagine a better parable for automation.

At first, the golem is created for convenience. It is not made to conquer the world. It is made to do housework. In modern terms, this is not the fantasy of artificial general intelligence taking over civilization. It is the humble promise of productivity software: let the machine handle the boring tasks. Let the assistant schedule the meeting, summarize the document, complete the form, write the report, answer the customer, process the claim, generate the code, reconcile the invoice, or order the lab test.

The danger begins not with evil, but with usefulness.

That is one of the reasons the golem remains such a powerful metaphor. It is not necessarily malicious. It does not hate its creator. It does not rebel in the dramatic, cinematic sense. Its threat comes from a mismatch between instruction and consequence. It acts, but it does not understand the full human context of its action. It obeys commands, but obedience is not wisdom.

This distinction is central to today’s AI agents.

A chatbot answers. An agent does. The difference is agency. Once a system can take actions across tools, send messages, modify files, place orders, move money, update records, or trigger workflows, the stakes change. We are no longer dealing only with text generation. We are dealing with delegated action.

Modern AI agents are animated by prompts, credentials, APIs, and permissions. The golem is animated by letters. The analogy is not exact, but it is revealing. In both cases, language is not just descriptive. Language becomes executable. A word does not merely represent reality. It changes reality.

In software, this is obvious. A command can delete a database. A script can move funds. A prompt can instruct an agent to email a client, update a spreadsheet, or deploy code. In the golem myth, the word *truth* gives life. Remove one letter, and the system shuts down. The ancient story treats language as an interface, a control layer, and a source of power.

That should feel familiar to anyone working with AI today.

We are surrounded by systems that turn language into action. “Summarize this.” “Book that.” “Generate this.” “Find the best option.” “Apply these rules.” “Use this patient context.” “Fill in the missing fields.” “Act on my behalf.” The interface becomes conversational, but the consequences are operational.

The golem’s forehead is, in a sense, the first dashboard. It displays the state of the system: alive or dead, active or inactive, *emes* or *meis*. But the story also warns us that a control mechanism is not enough if it becomes unreachable. The rabbi technically has a shutdown procedure. He knows exactly what to do. The problem is that the system has grown beyond the scale at which that procedure is safe.

This is one of the central challenges of automation: scale changes risk.

A safe agent is not only one that performs well when everything goes right. It is one that can be paused, audited, corrected, constrained, and shut down without causing more harm. The kill switch cannot be ornamental. It cannot depend on heroic intervention. It cannot require the creator to stand directly under the collapsing machine.

Good automation requires graceful failure.

The golem story also challenges the romance of “truth” in technology. The creature is animated by *emes*, truth. That feels noble. Today, we also surround AI with the language of truth: accuracy, intelligence, reasoning, evidence, confidence, alignment. But truth, in the story, is not enough. The word on the forehead gives the golem power, but not judgment. It creates action, but not ethics. It produces service, but not accountability.

That distinction matters. A system can be built on true data and still cause harm. It can follow rules and still fail people. It can optimize a task while damaging the larger process. It can be correct in a narrow sense and wrong in a human one.

This is why the golem is such a useful ancestor for the AI agent. It reminds us that the central problem is not simply whether artificial servants can work. The central problem is whether we understand what we are delegating, how far the delegation extends, and what happens when the servant’s power exceeds the creator’s reach.

The golem is not just a monster story. It is a governance story.

It asks: Who is responsible for the actions of a created agent? What limits should be placed on delegated power? How do we encode values into systems that cannot truly understand values? How do we prevent useful servants from becoming unmanageable infrastructure? How do we design off switches before we need them?

Four hundred years later, these questions are no longer folklore. They are product requirements.

Every AI agent needs boundaries. Every automation needs observability. Every autonomous workflow needs permissions, logs, escalation paths, and human override. Every system that can act should also be able to explain what it did, why it did it, and how to reverse it. The lesson of the golem is not “do not create.” Human beings will always create tools, servants, machines, and agents. The lesson is that creation without control is not innovation. It is negligence with better branding.
