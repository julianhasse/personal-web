The screen prompts are getting subtler, but the underlying mechanic is always the same. We see it every time we try to book a flight or buy a concert ticket: a tiny, pre-checked box or a soft-purple button inviting us to upgrade our experience. For an extra forty dollars, you can secure peace of mind. For an extra forty dollars, you can ensure a machine double-checks your results.

![](https://substack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com/public/images/c25ee068-c767-4654-8398-de4345195461_1404x2196.png)

As a designer working inside the complex plumbing of healthcare systems, watching this retail-style upselling creep into clinical workflows is deeply unsettling. It forces us to confront a heavy question about the future of patient experience: Are we turning advanced medical technology into a premium luxury feature?

A recent report highlighted this exact shift happening on the ground. Patients at a radiology clinic in Manhattan, and others at a facility in suburban Baltimore, were met with an unexpected choice at check-in. *They were asked to pay an out-of-pocket fee of forty dollars for an AI-assisted analysis of their mammograms. Because insurance does not cover the service, the cost falls squarely on the individual.*

From a technical and administrative perspective, it is easy to see how we got here. The algorithms are real, and they work. We have seen the data from clinical environments across Sweden and the United States showing that AI-assisted screening significantly optimizes workflows. In oncology, machine learning models are accelerating radiotherapy planning for complex lung, prostate, and colorectal cancers, cutting down on administrative friction and freeing up clinician hours. But implementing, validating, and maintaining these advanced tools requires significant capital. Hospitals and private practices are facing massive infrastructure costs to keep these systems running, and they are looking for a way to balance the ledger.

But from a human experience perspective, putting this choice on the patient feels like a massive design failure.

Good interface design in healthcare is supposed to reduce cognitive load, not increase it. It should build trust and clarity during moments that are already thick with anxiety. When a person sits down to fill out their intake forms before a cancer screening, they are already carrying a heavy emotional burden. Presenting them with a line-item upcharge for a secondary AI review introduces an unfair, stressful dilemma.

The underlying message we are accidentally sending to the patient is terrifying. We are implicitly telling them that standard care is somehow lesser, or that they have to pay a premium to unlock the safest, most accurate version of their diagnostic journey. If a patient cannot afford the extra forty dollars, they are forced to leave the clinic wondering if they missed a critical layer of protection. That is not just bad user experience; it is a system that compromises equity under the guise of innovation.

The medical community has moved past the debate of whether machine learning belongs in the exam room. It is already here, streamlining logistics, refining diagnostics, and reshaping patient communication. The real challenge now lies in how we weave these tools into standard practice without creating a multi-tiered system of care.

If an AI tool is effective enough to improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce physician burnout, it should not be treated as a line-item accessory like a premium streaming subscription. It needs to be integrated natively into standard care pathways, funded through broader operational efficiencies or systemic reimbursement models rather than individual microtransactions.

As we continue to build the interfaces and workflows that bridge the gap between complex clinical technology and human beings, we have to advocate for the person sitting on the other side of the glass. Innovation should elevate the baseline of care for everyone, not just for those who can afford to check the box.
